CHAPTER 1
INTRODUCTION

1.1 Overview of the Project

Vote Anywhere is a web-based simulation of a remote polling workflow for geographically mobile voters. It is a full-stack application developed as a final-year BCA project, and its purpose is to demonstrate, in working software, how an electoral administration could allow an eligible voter who is away from their registered constituency to vote at an authorised alternative polling centre — while preserving the two properties that make such a scheme defensible: that the voter receives the ballot of the constituency in which they are actually enrolled, and that nobody, including the operators of the system, can learn how any individual voted.

The system models five roles. A Voter authenticates with a mock one-time password, checks their eligibility, submits a remote polling request naming an alternative polling centre and a reason, and later casts a controlled ballot. A Verification Officer claims requests from a queue, reviews them, and approves or rejects them with a recorded reason. A Polling Centre Officer performs pre-vote verification at a centre, confirming that an arriving voter holds a valid assignment. An Election Administrator configures elections, activates and closes them, and reads aggregate reports. A System Auditor reads the audit trail and the post-closure reconciliation report in read-only mode. Every one of these interactions is a real HTTP request against a real database; none of them is a mock-up in the interface.

The application is organised as a modular monolith: a single Express service, internally divided into nine domain modules, sitting behind a single-page React frontend, with PostgreSQL 16 beneath it. The choice of a monolith over microservices is deliberate and is discussed in Section 4.2. The system needs to be comprehensible to an examiner and maintainable by one developer; a service mesh would add operational surface without adding a single enforceable invariant.

What distinguishes this project from a generic election CRUD application is that its central claims are enforced by construction rather than by convention. The claim that a remote polling centre does not change a voter’s constituency is enforced by the absence of any constituency field on the assignment record and by the type signature of the ballot service. The claim that a vote cannot be linked to a voter is enforced by the absence of any join between the two tables that would be needed to make that link. The claim that a voter cannot vote twice is enforced by a unique database constraint that holds even if two requests arrive in the same millisecond. These are design decisions, and the report treats them as the substance of the project.

Academic disclaimer. All voters, constituencies, polling centres, candidates, officer accounts and election data are fictional and are generated by a seed script. The application does not connect to the electoral roll of any country, to the Election Commission of India, to Aadhaar, to any biometric service or to any government API, and it does not conduct an election. It is a workflow simulator for academic assessment.

1.1.1 What the Application Actually Does

It is worth stating the workflow plainly before the surrounding analysis. A voter opens the application and selects the Voter tab on the sign-in screen. They enter their voter number, receive a six-digit one-time code through the development channel, and exchange it for a session token. On the dashboard they see their registered constituency, their home polling centre, the current election and its lifecycle state, and their own latest request with its status. They may then run an eligibility check, which reports four separate conditions and whether each passed.

If the election is still in the CONFIGURED state, the voter may submit a remote polling request. They choose a polling centre from a list; each entry is labelled with the constituency that centre belongs to. The service refuses a centre that lies inside the voter’s own registered constituency, because voting at one’s home centre is not remote polling and needs no permission. It also refuses a second active request, and this refusal is enforced by a partial unique index rather than by a read-then-write check in application code.

On the officer side, a Verification Officer sees a review queue filtered by status. Claiming a request moves it from PENDING to UNDER_REVIEW and records the reviewer. Approving it creates a RemotePollingAssignment in the same database transaction; an approved request with no assignment would leave a voter at a centre with nothing to verify against, so the two writes either both land or neither does. Rejecting it requires a non-empty reason, which is stored and shown to the voter.

Once the administrator activates the election, the ballot becomes available. The controlled ballot page lists the candidates standing in the voter’s registered constituency — not the remote centre’s — and, if the voter holds an assignment, displays the remote centre as a location. Submitting a choice seals it under AES-256-GCM, writes both sides of the secrecy boundary inside one transaction, and returns only an opaque ballot reference. A second submission is refused by the database.

After the administrator closes the election, two read-only reports become available. The reconciliation report compares, per constituency, the number of ballots issued against the number recorded and reports any discrepancy as a discrepancy rather than attempting to resolve it by matching individuals — because that match is precisely what ballot secrecy forbids. The results report decrypts each ballot inside the Election Simulator and produces aggregate counts per candidate per constituency.

1.1.2 Intended Readership

This report is written for four audiences. The project examiner will want Chapter 3 (analysis), Chapter 4 (design) and Chapter 7 (testing) to demonstrate a defensible engineering process. The external examiner at the viva will most likely ask why the system cannot link a vote to a voter, and Chapter 4, Section 4.6 and Appendix A answer that question from the schema upwards. A future maintainer will find Chapter 5 and Chapter 6 a guided tour of the codebase, and Appendix B a complete reference for the thirty-one REST endpoints. A reviewer assessing the academic claim will find Chapter 2 positioning the work against existing systems and Chapter 8 stating plainly what the system does not do.

1.2 Background of the Study

Every large democracy has a structural tension between the locality of representation and the mobility of the population it represents. Parliamentary and assembly constituencies are defined geographically. A voter is enrolled in the constituency where they are ordinarily resident, and that enrolment determines which candidates appear on their ballot. Because the ballot is a contest between candidates standing in a particular constituency, it follows that a voter cannot simply be handed a different constituency’s ballot on the day of the poll without changing the composition of the electorate that elects that constituency’s representative.

At the same time, internal migration is large. In India, the Census of India has consistently recorded a very substantial volume of intra-state and inter-state movement driven by employment, marriage, education and family circumstance. A large share of this movement is circular rather than permanent: a worker may spend most of the year in a city while retaining a family home and an enrolment in a distant district. The COVID-19 period made the scale of this population visible in a way that earlier statistics had not.

The consequence for electoral administration is a group of voters who are legally entitled to vote, who are correctly enrolled, and who are physically unable to reach their assigned polling station within the polling window. Electoral commissions have responded with a family of measures: postal ballots for specified categories such as service voters and electors on election duty, proxy voting in some jurisdictions, extended polling hours, and, in a small number of countries, arrangements for voting at any polling station within a defined area.

The last of these — remote or out-of-constituency voting — is the most general and the most difficult. It is difficult not because transporting a ballot to another location is technically hard, but because the moment a ballot is cast away from the constituency it belongs to, three problems appear at once. First, the ballot must still be the correct ballot: the candidates on it must be those of the voter’s registered constituency, or the election result is corrupted. Second, the secrecy of the ballot must survive the journey: at a remote centre, an official who knows the voter’s identity is standing next to the ballot, so any design that stores an identifiable ballot undermines the secret ballot. Third, the administration must be able to demonstrate afterwards that the number of ballots issued equals the number recorded, without being able to demonstrate who cast which ballot.

These three problems are the design centre of this project. The application is small — a handful of constituencies and a few hundred lines of domain logic — but the invariants it makes visible are the same ones that make real remote voting hard.

1.2.1 The Indian Electoral Context

India’s electoral machinery is unusually centralised in its standards and unusually distributed in its execution. The Election Commission of India sets the rules and the timetable; the actual poll is run by district administration through lakhs of polling stations, each staffed by a presiding officer and polling officers drawn from other government departments. A voter is assigned to one polling station within their constituency and appears there in person, presents identification, is marked on the electoral roll, and proceeds to the ballot unit.

Three features of this arrangement shape the present design. Verification is physical. The identity check happens at a counter, by a person, against a printed roll. A remote polling scheme therefore cannot be purely digital: it must produce a document, or a record, that an officer at the remote centre can check in person. Vote Anywhere models this with the RemotePollingAssignment, and the Polling Centre Officer’s pre-vote verification screen is the digital analogue of that counter.

The ballot is constituency-specific. In a general election a voter in Chennai South receives a ballot listing the candidates for Chennai South. There is no national list. This is what makes the Core Rule of this project a real constraint rather than a stylistic preference: if a remote polling centre in Madurai were allowed to determine the ballot, a Chennai South voter appearing in Madurai would be handed a Madurai Central ballot and would be voting in an election they are not enrolled in.

The audit trail is paper. Presiding officers complete Form 17A, statutory accounts, and a series of declarations; the paper trail is the evidence base for a challenge. Digitising any part of this requires an equivalent, and that equivalent is the AuditRecord table: append-only, written as a side effect of every state change, never editable through an API.

1.2.2 Why Simulation Rather Than a Real System

A student project cannot integrate with a national electoral roll, cannot obtain identity data, and must not handle real personal information. Three options were available: build a prototype that pretends loudly and breaks under questioning, build a purely paper design, or build a simulator with fictional data whose internal consistency is genuine. The third option was chosen. The consequence is that every claim in this report is about the behaviour of the software under fictional data, and none of it is a claim about electoral law. The benefit is that the invariants can be tested honestly — there are twenty-eight automated tests and each one asserts something that an examiner can reproduce.

1.2.3 Balloting Technology That This Project Does Not Use

Because the words “electronic voting” and “blockchain” appear so often in student projects that they have become nearly meaningless, it is worth being explicit about what this system is not. It is not an electronic voting machine; no ballot is cast through it that counts towards any result other than a simulated aggregate. It does not use a distributed ledger. It does not implement homomorphic tallying, zero-knowledge proofs, mix-nets or verifiable shuffle. It does not use a hardware security module; the encryption key lives in an environment variable and the report says so plainly in Section 8.3.

What it does use is a small number of well-understood primitives applied carefully: bcrypt for password and one-time-code hashing, AES-256-GCM for authenticating a sealed ballot, a cryptographically secure random number generator for every identifier and nonce, and a relational database with real constraints for the invariants that must survive concurrency. The intellectual content of the project lies not in exotic cryptography but in the placement of these primitives: the decision that the secrecy boundary is a schema boundary, and the decision that the Core Rule is enforced by a function signature rather than by a comment.

This restraint is itself an argument. A great many academic proposals for election technology fail not because their cryptography is weak but because their architecture makes an invariant unstateable. If the constituency can be supplied by the caller, no amount of encryption will keep the correct ballot on the correct screen. Vote Anywhere was built from the invariant outwards.

1.3 Problem Statement

The problem addressed by this project can be stated in one sentence:

“There is no lightweight, auditable workflow that lets an enrolled voter who is physically away from their registered constituency vote at an authorised alternative polling centre, while guaranteeing that they receive their own constituency’s ballot, that their choice cannot be linked to their identity, and that the administration can afterwards reconcile the number of ballots issued against the number recorded.”

Each clause of that statement identifies a distinct failure mode in the informal alternatives. The clauses are examined below, and the specific problems that the design must answer are enumerated.

1.3.1 Enumerated Problems

Problem 1 — The ballot-attribution problem. If a voter appears at an alternative centre, whatever system serves them must know which ballot to present. The obvious implementation — take the constituency from the centre the voter is standing in — is wrong, and wrong in a way that silently corrupts results: the voter’s choice would be counted in a constituency where they are not enrolled. The correct source of the constituency is the voter record and nothing else. The design problem is how to make that the only possible source, rather than the intended one.

Problem 2 — The ballot-secrecy problem. A remote voting scheme brings identity and choice into physical proximity. If the database stores a voter identifier beside a candidate choice, then anyone with database access — or anyone who can obtain a report, a log line or an API response — can reconstruct how an individual voted. The design problem is to represent “this voter has voted” and “this choice was made” in a way that no query can join, including queries written by a future maintainer who has never read this report.

Problem 3 — The duplicate-ballot problem. A check of the form “read whether the voter has voted, then write the ballot if not” is incorrect under concurrency. Two requests arriving together can both read “not voted” and both proceed. The design problem is to place the guarantee where it cannot be raced — in the database, not in the service layer.

Problem 4 — The request-lifecycle problem. Approval is not a single flag. A request moves through a defined set of states, and several moves that an implementer might plausibly allow are indefensible: a rejected request must not later be approved without a new application; an approved request must not return to pending; a used request must not be reopened. The design problem is to make every illegal move impossible in one place rather than to scatter conditionals across the code.

Problem 5 — The accountability problem. Every state change must be attributable to an actor and a time, but the audit trail must not itself become a leak. The design problem is to record enough to reconstruct the administration of the poll while recording nothing that reveals a choice or a credential.

1.3.2 Why the Informal Alternatives Fail

Before the software design is considered, it is worth asking why the problem is not already solved by administrative practice. Four informal answers are commonly proposed, and each fails for a specific reason.

Postal balloting. Postal ballots are used in India for defined categories of elector. They work, but they are slow, they require a reliable postal address, and they move the secrecy problem rather than solving it: a postal ballot arrives at a counting centre inside an envelope that bears, at some point in its journey, both an identity and a choice. The covering envelope and the ballot are separated by procedure and by physical handling. Software has no equivalent of “separate the envelope in a locked room” unless the data model is designed to make the separation structural.

Proxy voting. A proxy solution replaces the absent voter with a present one. It solves the travel problem and abandons the personal-vote principle, and it requires a trusted relationship that cannot be verified by a system.

Extended polling windows. Giving the voter more time helps only if the obstacle is time. For a voter two states away, the obstacle is distance, and an extra four hours does not remove it.

Informal local arrangements. The most common practical answer is a phone call and an informal permission. This is precisely what must not happen in an election: it produces no record, no audit, no reconciliation, and no defence if the result is challenged. The existence of informal arrangements is the strongest argument for a formal workflow, because a formal workflow is at least auditable.

1.4 Objectives of the Project

The objectives are divided into primary objectives, which the system must meet for the project to be considered complete, and secondary objectives, which improve the quality of the deliverable without being essential to it. Each primary objective is stated with the evidence that demonstrates it.

1.4.1 Primary Objectives

  1. To model the complete remote polling workflow end to end. Authentication, eligibility verification, request submission, officer review, approval or rejection, assignment, ballot presentation, ballot submission, reconciliation and results must all be implemented as working code paths that operate on persistent data. Evidence: Chapter 6, Section 6.10 (integration of modules), and the integration tests in Appendix C.
  2. To enforce the Core Rule structurally. The voter’s registered constituency must be the sole input to ballot generation, and the approved assignment must not carry a constituency at all. Evidence: Section 4.6.3 and the ballot generation regression test.
  3. To separate identity from choice at the level of the schema. Two disjoint tables, no relation, no shared key. Evidence: Appendix A and the schema-inspection test.
  4. To demonstrate ballot integrity using authenticated encryption. A submitted ballot must be sealed with AES-256-GCM, bound to its context, and verifiable without exposing the plaintext. Evidence: Sections 6.6 and 7.5.
  5. To prevent duplicate ballot submission at the database level. Evidence: Section 6.6.4 and the concurrency test.
  6. To implement role-based authorization on the server. Every route must declare its permitted roles explicitly, with deny-by-default semantics; hiding a page in the browser must not be the control. Evidence: Section 6.3 and the authorization test suite.
  7. To record every state-changing operation in an append-only audit trail. Evidence: Section 6.8 and the audit tests.
  8. To implement a single-entry-per-use mock OTP for voters and a hashed password for officers. Evidence: Section 6.2.
  9. To provide a reproducible development environment. A documented sequence of commands must produce a running system on a fresh machine. Evidence: Section 5.5 and the repository README.
  10. To test the invariants, not only the happy paths. Every illegal transition, every duplicate, every cross-constituency candidate and every tampered ciphertext must have a test that proves it is refused. Evidence: Chapter 7 and Appendix C.

1.4.2 Secondary Objectives

  1. To present a coherent, role-aware interface. Each portal should show only the navigation relevant to the signed-in role, and should explain the workflow rather than merely expose endpoints. The voter portal uses a stepper to show where a request sits in the lifecycle.
  2. To make the system self-explanatory to a reviewer. The Prisma schema carries a comment on every model that states the invariant it protects, so that a reader who opens one file learns the design.
  3. To produce documentation of professional standard. This report, the REST reference in Appendix B and the data dictionary in Appendix A form a single consistent documentation set, generated from the same understanding of the code.
  4. To keep dependencies conservative. The backend uses eleven direct runtime dependencies and the frontend eight. Where a library would have added a hundred lines of configuration for ten lines of clarity, it was not adopted.
  5. To fail safely and fail visibly. A missing or malformed encryption key must stop the system rather than cause silent corruption; a database constraint violation must become a clean domain error rather than a leaked constraint name.
  6. To write the code so that it can be read. Services orchestrate and repositories persist; there is no Prisma call outside a repository; no HTTP concern inside a service.

1.4.3 Explicit Non-Objectives

It is as important to state what was deliberately not attempted, because a report that claims everything proves nothing. The following were excluded by decision rather than by omission:

  • Integration with any real identity system — Aadhaar, voter ID verification, biometric matching or government single sign-on. Excluded because the project handles fictional data and must not process personal information.
  • Cryptographic verifiability of the result — zero-knowledge proofs, mix-nets, end-to-end verifiable tallies. Excluded because they require a mathematical development well beyond the scope of a BCA project, and because a half-implemented end-to-end verification scheme is more dangerous than an honest simulator.
  • Distributed consensus — there is one server and one database. The report does not use the words “consensus”, “peer” or “quorum” to describe anything in this system, because the system has none of them.
  • Production hardening — key rotation, hardware security modules, multi-region failover, penetration testing, load testing beyond a smoke level.
  • Accessibility conformance — the interface follows ordinary good practice but has not been audited against WCAG. Section 8.4 notes this as future work.
  • Operational integrations — SMS or email delivery of the one-time code, statutory reporting formats, and integration with a real electoral roll.

1.5.2 Out of Scope

The following are outside the boundary. Where an item is partly present, the limitation is stated precisely rather than glossed.

  • Real identity verification. No Aadhaar, no biometric capture, no electoral-roll lookup, no address proof, no document upload. A voter is identified by a fictional voter number issued by the seed script.
  • Real one-time-code delivery. The OTP is written to the server log with an explicit [DEV ONLY] prefix in development and returned in the response body only when NODE_ENV is test. No SMS gateway or mail provider is integrated.
  • Multi-election operation. The service uses the concept of “the current election”. The schema supports many elections and the administrator sees them all, but a voter’s dashboard and the ballot surface resolve a single current election. Concurrent elections for different constituencies are not modelled.
  • Ballot paper fidelity. The ballot lists candidate name and symbol. Party affiliation, symbols as images, NOTA, and multi-seat or preferential voting are absent.
  • Write-side administration. Elections, candidates, polling centres, constituencies and voters are created by the seed script, not by the interface. The administrator activates and closes elections and reads reports but does not create them through the API.
  • Cryptographic end-to-end verifiability. A ballot can be shown to be unmodified, and a reconciliation discrepancy can be reported. The system does not produce a cryptographic proof that the published tally matches the recorded ballots.
  • Distributed deployment. One backend process, one database. No horizontal scaling, no message broker, no cache tier.
  • Full container orchestration. Only PostgreSQL runs in a container at this stage. Development images for the backend and frontend are identified as a later phase in Section 5.5.

1.5.3 Scope Boundary Rationale

Three principles governed where the line was drawn. First, prefer an invariant over a feature. Given limited time, implementing a second report type adds little; implementing the database constraint that makes duplicate ballots impossible adds the thing the project is about. Second, never half-implement a security mechanism. A partial zero-knowledge scheme would be worse than none, because it would invite a claim the code cannot support. Third, keep the fictional boundary visible. Everything that would require real personal data was excluded, so that the system can be demonstrated in a viva without any privacy obligation.

1.6 Project Motivation

The motivation for this project was threefold: a civic observation, an engineering interest, and an academic requirement. They are set out separately because they produced different design decisions.

1.6.1 Civic Motivation

Disenfranchisement by distance is a quiet form of exclusion. It produces no protest because the people affected are, by definition, not in the place where the poll is happening, and it is rarely counted because the denominator — the number of enrolled voters who could not reach their station — is not measured. A student who has moved to Chennai from another district for a three-year degree faces the same choice every election year: travel several hundred kilometres, or do not vote. Many do not vote.

The project does not claim to solve this. A simulator changes nothing about electoral law. But a simulator can make the problem legible, and it can show that the two objections most often raised against remote voting — that it would corrupt the constituency result, and that it would destroy ballot secrecy — are engineering problems with engineering answers, not fatal objections. That, at least, is a contribution a student project can honestly make.

1.6.2 Engineering Motivation

The technical interest lay in a specific class of problem: invariants that must survive other people’s future code. A comment saying “do not pass the remote constituency here” is a wish. A function that cannot accept a constituency at all is a guarantee. Most of the design effort in this project went into converting wishes into guarantees.

Three conversions were particularly instructive. The first was moving duplicate detection from an if statement into a unique index, which required an excursion into partial indexes because the uniqueness only applies to active requests. The second was realising that an approved request and its assignment must be written in one transaction, because an approved request without an assignment is a state in which a voter exists who is permitted to vote remotely but cannot be verified at the centre. The third was separating ballot verification from ballot counting into two functions with different return types, so that the verification path has no type-level way to leak a choice.

The project also exercised a large part of the BCA syllabus in one artefact: relational design and normalisation, SQL constraints and indexes, a typed service layer, HTTP semantics and status codes, client-side routing and form validation, cryptographic hashing, configuration management, containerisation, and systematic testing. The report deliberately documents these connections rather than treating the stack as a list of logos.

1.6.3 Academic Motivation

As a final-year BCA project, the work had to demonstrate proficiency across the programme: database design, software engineering method, web development, and the ability to document a system to a professional standard. The choice of a problem with strong invariants made the engineering method visible. It is easy to write a report about a CRUD application; it is much easier to demonstrate analysis and design when the application has a property that can be violated, and therefore a property that must be argued for, tested, and defended at a viva.

1.7 Assumptions and Constraints

The system is correct only within its assumptions. Stating them is therefore part of stating what the system does.

1.7.1 Assumptions

  1. The voter record is authoritative. The registered constituency stored on the voter record is assumed correct and is never re-derived. No component attempts to infer a constituency from an address, a centre or a location.
  2. Officers are trusted to follow procedure. The system records decisions and reasons but cannot verify that a Verification Officer read the evidence before approving. It can only make the decision attributable.
  3. An approved assignment is honoured physically. The system assumes that a Polling Centre Officer will check the assignment before permitting a ballot. The application supports that check with a verification screen; it cannot compel it.
  4. The clock is monotonic enough. Expiry of sessions and one-time codes uses server time. A grossly incorrect server clock would break expiry without breaking anything else.
  5. A single administrative domain exists. The system assumes one election authority, one electoral roll and one set of constituencies. It does not model federated authorities.
  6. The browser is modern and the network is available. The frontend targets current evergreen browsers and assumes a working connection; there is no offline mode.
  7. Development secrets are adequate for development. The encryption key and JWT secret in .env are development values. The report assumes no adversary with access to the development environment, and says so rather than implying production readiness.
  8. Fictional data is internally consistent. Every voter, centre and candidate is invented, but the relationships between them are real, which is what allows the invariants to be tested.

1.7.2 Technical Constraints

  • Single-key ballot encryption. One AES-256 key protects all ballots. Rotating the key makes previously sealed ballots unreadable. This is documented behaviour, not a defect discovered late.
  • Prisma cannot express partial indexes. The constraint that permits at most one active request per voter per election is hand-written SQL inside a migration. It is therefore invisible to Prisma’s schema-diffing and must be maintained by hand.
  • Audit records are not tamper-evident. They are append-only by construction (no update or delete path exists) but are not hash-chained. A party with direct database access could alter them.
  • Counting is in-process. Decryption of every ballot happens in the application. At the scale of a national election this would be untenable; at the scale of a demonstration it is instantaneous.
  • No horizontal scaling. Rate limiting is in-process memory, so a second backend replica would double the effective limit.
  • Tests require a live database. The suites run against a real PostgreSQL 16 instance rather than an in-memory substitute, because the constraints being tested are database features.

1.7.3 Operational Constraints

  • Single developer, single academic term. The project was implemented by one student within one semester, which is why write-side administration of elections and candidates is seed-driven rather than interface-driven.
  • No access to real electoral data. By design and by law, all data is invented.
  • Assessment environment. The demonstration runs on a developer laptop with Docker for PostgreSQL; no cloud infrastructure is assumed.

1.8 Organization of the Report

This report is organised into eight chapters and four appendices. The structure follows the software development life cycle used in the project: a waterfall of analysis and design followed by iterative implementation and testing.

Chapter 1 — Introduction. States the problem, the objectives, the scope, the motivation, and the assumptions and constraints within which the system is correct.

Chapter 2 — Literature Review. Reviews existing approaches to remote and out-of-constituency voting, examines the technology families that academic projects in this area commonly adopt, compares them in a structured table, and identifies the gaps that this project addresses.

Chapter 3 — System Analysis. Analyses the existing manual and semi-manual process, states its limitations, describes the proposed system and its advantages, presents the three feasibility studies, and closes with the software requirements specification, including functional requirements, non-functional requirements, use-case descriptions and business rules.

Chapter 4 — System Design. Presents the architecture, the module decomposition, the data flow diagrams at three levels, the use-case diagram, the class diagram, the entity-relationship diagram, the complete database design including every table and constraint, and the input and output design. Sections 4.6.3 and 4.7 are the sections an examiner should read first, because the Core Rule and the secrecy boundary are design decisions, not implementation details.
