ABSTRACT


Food waste is one of the most pressing global challenges of the 21st century. According to the United Nations Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO), approximately one-third of all food produced globally is wasted annually, amounting to roughly 1.3 billion tonnes. Meanwhile, millions of people go to bed hungry every night. Old age homes, orphanages, and other social institutions often struggle to provide adequate meals for their residents.

The Waste Food Management with Provider, Volunteer and Receiver is a web-based application developed using Python Flask framework that bridges the gap between surplus food sources (restaurants, event caterers, wedding halls) and those who need it (old age homes, orphanages, homeless shelters). The system facilitates a three-role architecture comprising Food Providers, Volunteers, and Food Needers.

When a food provider posts available surplus food on the platform, all registered volunteers receive real-time notifications. Volunteers can then pick up the food and deliver it to registered food needers based on location and head count requirements. The system supports partial deliveries, splitting large food donations across multiple receivers, and provides complete end-to-end tracking from food posting to receipt confirmation.

Key features include role-based access control, real-time notification system, partial delivery support, request cancellation with automatic food reassignment, and responsive user interface design using Bootstrap 5. The application uses SQLite as the database engine through SQLAlchemy ORM, Flask-Login for session management, and Werkzeug for secure password hashing.

The system has been successfully tested across all user flows and demonstrates a practical, deployable solution for reducing food wastage while feeding those in need.


Keywords: Food Waste Management, Web Application, Python Flask, SQLite, Role-Based Access Control, Real-Time Notifications, Volunteer Coordination, Food Redistribution.

INTRODUCTION

1.1 Overview of the Project

The Waste Food Management with Provider, Volunteer and Receiver is a comprehensive web-based application designed to address the critical challenge of food wastage in India and worldwide. The system creates a digital bridge connecting three key stakeholders in the food redistribution chain: Food Providers (restaurants, hotels, event organizers), Volunteers (delivery coordinators), and Food Needers (old age homes, orphanages, shelters).

The application is developed using Python Flask, a lightweight yet powerful micro-framework that follows the Model-View-Controller (MVC) architecture pattern. The backend leverages SQLAlchemy ORM for database operations with SQLite as the storage engine, while the frontend employs Bootstrap 5 for responsive design and Jinja2 for server-side template rendering.

The core workflow of the system operates as follows: When a food provider has surplus food (after an event, restaurant closing hours, or excess preparation), they post the details including food description, quantity, number of persons it can serve, and pickup location. All registered volunteers are immediately notified of the new posting. A volunteer can then pick the food, choose a suitable receiver from the list of registered food needers, and manage the delivery process. The food needer confirms receipt upon delivery, completing the food redistribution cycle.

A unique feature of this system is its support for partial deliveries. If a food post can serve 100 persons but a particular needer only needs food for 30, the volunteer can deliver 30 servings while the remaining 70 remain available for other volunteers to pick up for different receivers. This maximizes food utilization and minimizes waste.

1.2 Background of the Study

Food waste is a global crisis of staggering proportions. The United Nations Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) reports that approximately 1.3 billion tonnes of food produced for human consumption is lost or wasted annually worldwide. This represents roughly one-third of all food produced globally. In India alone, the Food Waste Index Report 2024 by UNEP estimates that 78.2 million tonnes of food is wasted annually, making India one of the largest contributors to food waste globally.

The tragedy is compounded by the fact that while enormous quantities of food are wasted, millions of people suffer from hunger and malnutrition. According to the Global Hunger Index 2023, India ranks 111th out of 125 countries, with a score that indicates a level of hunger that is serious. Old age homes, orphanages, and homeless shelters across the country struggle daily to provide adequate meals for their residents.

The disconnect between food surplus and food scarcity exists primarily due to logistical challenges. Restaurants, wedding halls, and event caterers often have large quantities of leftover food at the end of service, but lack the means or motivation to redistribute it before spoilage. The perishable nature of prepared food demands immediate action — typically within 2-4 hours of preparation for safe consumption.

Several social initiatives and NGOs have attempted to bridge this gap through manual coordination via phone calls, WhatsApp groups, and social media posts. However, these informal methods suffer from poor scalability, lack of systematic tracking, absence of real-time coordination, and inability to handle partial distributions efficiently.

The emergence of web technologies and smartphone proliferation has created an opportunity to build systematic, scalable platforms for food redistribution. This project leverages these technologies to create an efficient, real-time coordination system that can operate at scale while maintaining simplicity of use.

1.3 Problem Statement

The fundamental problem addressed by this project can be stated as follows:

"There exists no efficient, centralized, real-time system to connect surplus food sources with food-needy institutions while coordinating volunteer delivery logistics, supporting partial food distribution, and providing end-to-end tracking of the food redistribution process."

The specific problems identified are:

  1. Information Asymmetry: Food providers do not know which institutions need food, and food needers do not know which providers have surplus. There is no common platform for visibility.
  2. Time Sensitivity: Prepared food has a limited shelf life. By the time manual coordination happens (through phone calls or social media), the food often becomes unsuitable for consumption.
  3. Volunteer Coordination: Even when willing volunteers exist, there is no systematic way to notify them of available food, assign them to deliveries, and track the delivery process.
  4. Partial Distribution: A wedding event may have food for 500 persons, but a single orphanage may need food for only 50. Existing systems cannot handle splitting a single food source across multiple receivers efficiently.
  5. Accountability Gap: Without tracking, there is no way to verify whether the food actually reached the intended recipients. This discourages repeated participation from providers.
  6. Scalability: Manual coordination methods (phone trees, WhatsApp groups) break down beyond a small community size. A digital platform is needed that can scale to city-wide or even state-wide operations.

  1. To develop a web-based platform that connects food providers, volunteers, and food needers in real-time for efficient food redistribution.

  3. To support partial food distribution enabling large food donations to be split across multiple receiving institutions based on their individual needs.

  9. To minimize food wastage by providing a fast, efficient channel for food redistribution before spoilage.
  10. To empower volunteer networks with tools for efficient pickup and delivery management.

2.2 Review of Existing Systems

2.2.1 Too Good To Go

Too Good To Go is a mobile application launched in Denmark in 2016 that allows restaurants and stores to sell surplus food at reduced prices. The platform operates in 17 countries across Europe and North America. Users purchase "surprise bags" of surplus food at one-third of the retail price.

Technology: Native mobile applications (iOS/Android) with cloud-based backend.

Strengths: Large user base, commercial sustainability through revenue sharing, GPS-based store locator.

Limitations: Commercial model – food is sold, not donated. Does not address institutional needs (orphanages, old age homes). No volunteer coordination for delivery. Not available in India.

2.2.2 Feeding India (Zomato)

Feeding India, now part of Zomato, is an Indian non-profit that facilitates food donation from restaurants, corporate events, and individuals to those in need. It operates through a network of volunteers across 200+ cities in India.

Technology: Web and mobile platform integrated with Zomato's infrastructure.

Strengths: Wide reach across India, established volunteer network, corporate backing.

Limitations: Primarily centralized coordination – not fully automated. Relies heavily on manual volunteer assignment. No partial delivery support. Limited transparency in delivery tracking for donors.

2.2.3 No Food Waste (India)

No Food Waste is a Chennai-based non-profit organization that collects excess food from events, restaurants, and households and redistributes it to orphanages, old age homes, and homeless people. Founded in 2014, it operates primarily through WhatsApp groups and phone calls.

Technology: Primarily manual coordination via WhatsApp and phone calls. Basic website for awareness.

Strengths: Strong local presence in Chennai, established relationships with institutions.

Limitations: Not scalable beyond local area. No real-time tracking. No automated volunteer assignment. No support for partial deliveries. Entirely manual process.

10

2.2.4 OLIO

OLIO is a free app for sharing surplus food between neighbors and local businesses. Founded in the UK in 2015, it operates as a peer-to-peer food sharing platform. Users photograph items they want to share, and neighbors can request items for pickup.

Technology: Native mobile apps with Node.js backend, MongoDB database, GPS integration.

Strengths: Peer-to-peer model encourages community participation. Handles both cooked and uncooked food. GPS-based proximity matching.

Limitations: Peer-to-peer only – no institutional support. No dedicated volunteer delivery system. Requires physical pickup by the receiver. Not designed for bulk food redistribution. Limited presence in India.

2.2.5 Robin Hood Army

Robin Hood Army is a volunteer-based organization operating in India and other South Asian countries that redistributes surplus food to the less fortunate. Founded in 2014, it has served over 65 million people across 200+ cities.

Technology: Primarily social media coordination (Facebook groups, WhatsApp). Mobile app for volunteer management.

Strengths: Large volunteer network, strong social media presence, operates across multiple cities.

Limitations: Technology is primarily for coordination awareness rather than real-time automated matching. No automated food-to-receiver matching. Manual volunteer assignment. No partial delivery tracking.

2.2.6 Food Rescue US

Food Rescue US is an American non-profit that uses technology to fight hunger and prevent food waste. Their platform connects food donors with recipient agencies through volunteer "food rescuers" who transport the food.

Technology: Web platform and mobile app with GPS tracking, automated volunteer matching.

Strengths: Automated matching algorithm, GPS-based routing, delivery confirmation, impact tracking.

Limitations: Operates only in the US. Designed for American food safety regulations. No partial delivery support. Heavy infrastructure requirements.

11

2.2.7 Goodr (Atlanta, USA)

Goodr is a sustainable waste management company that leverages technology to address food waste and hunger. It uses blockchain technology to track food from donation to delivery, ensuring accountability and transparency.

Technology: Web platform with blockchain tracking, IoT sensors for food quality monitoring.

Strengths: Blockchain ensures transparency and accountability. IoT sensors monitor food temperature during transport. Real-time tracking. Tax receipt generation for donors.

Limitations: Enterprise-focused, not suitable for individual donors. Very high implementation complexity. Not available outside the US. Requires significant infrastructure (IoT devices, blockchain nodes).

2.2.8 Academic Research

Several academic papers have proposed food waste management systems:

Kumar et al. (2021) proposed a "Food Waste Management System using IoT" that uses sensors to detect food waste levels in commercial kitchens. However, this focuses on waste measurement rather than redistribution.

Sharma and Gupta (2022) developed a "Smart Food Donation Platform using Machine Learning" that predicts surplus food quantities. While innovative, the prediction component adds complexity without directly solving the coordination problem.

Patel et al. (2023) described a "Cloud-Based Food Redistribution Network" using microservices architecture. The system is technically sophisticated but over-engineered for the basic coordination needs of food redistribution in developing countries.

Ramesh and Krishnan (2023) proposed a "Mobile-First Food Sharing Application" for Indian cities using React Native and Firebase. While targeting the Indian context, the system lacks volunteer coordination and partial delivery features.

2.2.9 Government Initiatives

The Indian government has taken several steps to address food waste:

Food Safety and Standards Authority of India (FSSAI) launched the "Save Food Share Food" initiative in 2017, creating a network of food banks across the country. However, the technology infrastructure for real-time coordination remains limited.

Indian Food Sharing Alliance (IFSA) was proposed as a platform connecting food businesses with charities. The initiative focuses on policy framework rather than technology implementation.

Annapurna Scheme provides food to senior citizens below the poverty line but operates through fixed distribution centers rather than dynamic redistribution.

While these government initiatives address food security at a policy level, they lack the real-time, technology-driven coordination capabilities needed for efficient surplus food redistribution. The gap between policy frameworks and ground-level implementation remains significant.

2.2.10 Technology Trends in Food Management

The literature reveals several emerging technology trends in food waste management:

  • IoT (Internet of Things): Sensors for monitoring food freshness, temperature, and waste levels
  • Blockchain: Transparent tracking of food from source to recipient
  • Machine Learning: Predicting surplus quantities and demand patterns
  • GPS/GIS: Location-based matching and route optimization for delivery
  • Cloud Computing: Scalable infrastructure for real-time coordination
  • Mobile-First Design: Ensuring accessibility on smartphones in developing countries

While these technologies offer powerful capabilities, their complexity and infrastructure requirements often make them unsuitable for lightweight, rapid-deployment solutions needed in resource-constrained environments. A simpler, web-based approach using proven technologies (Python, Flask, SQLite) offers faster deployment and easier maintenance.

2.4 Research Gaps Identified

Based on the comprehensive literature review conducted, the following research gaps were identified that the proposed system aims to address:

Gap 1: Lack of Partial Delivery Support

None of the reviewed systems support splitting a single food source across multiple receivers based on their individual needs. In practice, a wedding event may produce surplus food for 200 persons, but individual institutions may need food for 20-50 persons each. Without partial delivery support, the remaining food goes to waste or the entire quantity must go to a single receiver regardless of their actual need.

How the proposed system addresses this: The food post maintains a "remaining persons" counter that decreases with each pickup. Multiple volunteers can pick different quantities from the same post for different receivers.

Gap 2: Absence of Automated Volunteer Coordination

Existing systems either assign volunteers manually (through coordinators) or require receivers to pick up food themselves. This creates bottlenecks and delays in the redistribution process.

3.1 Existing System Analysis

The existing system for food redistribution in India operates primarily through manual, informal channels. The current process typically involves the following steps:

Manual Coordination Process:

  1. A food provider (restaurant, wedding hall, event organizer) recognizes surplus food at the end of service.
  2. The provider contacts known NGOs or volunteer groups via phone calls or WhatsApp messages.
  3. The NGO coordinator manually identifies available volunteers and contacts them individually.
  4. A volunteer agrees to pick up the food and is given the provider's address.
  5. The coordinator separately identifies a suitable receiver (orphanage, old age home) based on memory or contact lists.
  6. The volunteer picks up food and delivers to the designated receiver.
  7. There is typically no formal confirmation or tracking of the delivery.

Characteristics of the Existing System:

  • Communication: Phone calls, WhatsApp messages, Facebook posts
  • Coordination: Single point coordinator (bottleneck)
  • Tracking: None or informal (photos shared on WhatsApp)
  • Data Storage: Contact lists on personal phones, no database
  • Response Time: 30 minutes to 2 hours (often too late for perishable food)
  • Scalability: Limited to coordinator's personal network
  • Partial Delivery: Not supported – entire quantity goes to one receiver
21

3.2 Limitations of Existing System

The manual food redistribution approach suffers from the following critical limitations:

1. Single Point of Failure (Coordinator Bottleneck)

The entire process depends on a single coordinator being available and responsive. If the coordinator is unavailable (night hours, holidays, personal commitments), the food goes to waste. There is no backup mechanism or automated failover.

2. Response Time Delay

The sequential nature of communication (provider → coordinator → volunteer → receiver) introduces significant delays. By the time all parties are coordinated, prepared food may no longer be safe for consumption. Studies show that cooked food should be consumed within 2-4 hours if not refrigerated.

3. No Real-Time Visibility

Providers cannot see how many volunteers are available. Volunteers do not know which food is available until personally contacted. Receivers have no visibility into incoming food. This information asymmetry leads to missed opportunities.

4. Inability to Handle Partial Distribution

A wedding generating surplus food for 300 persons must find a single receiver who can accept all 300 servings. If no single institution needs that quantity, the food goes to waste. There is no mechanism to split the surplus across multiple smaller institutions.

5. No Cancellation Recovery

If a receiver's plans change after food is picked up (emergency, already received food from another source), the volunteer has no mechanism to redirect the food to another receiver quickly.

6. No Historical Data or Accountability

Without systematic recording, there is no way to track how much food has been saved, which volunteers are most active, or which receivers have received food. This makes reporting to donors and stakeholders impossible.

7. Not Scalable

Manual coordination works for 5-10 transactions per week within a small community. It completely breaks down at 50+ transactions across a city. Each new provider or receiver requires manual onboarding into personal contact lists.

4.2 System Architecture

The Waste Food Management with Provider, Volunteer and Receiver follows a three-tier architecture pattern consisting of the Presentation Layer, Application (Business Logic) Layer, and Data Layer. This architecture provides clear separation of concerns and enables independent modification of each layer.

LayerTechnologyResponsibility

Data LayerSQLAlchemy ORM, SQLiteData persistence, queries, transactions

  2. Flask routes the request to appropriate view function (Application Layer)

6.2 Algorithm Description

The following algorithms describe the core business logic of the system:

Algorithm 1: Food Posting with Broadcast Notification

ALGORITHM: PostFood
INPUT: food_description, quantity, persons, location, contact
OUTPUT: Food post created, all volunteers notified

BEGIN
    1. Validate input fields (all required, persons > 0)
    2. Create FoodPost record:
       - user_id = current_user.id
       - remaining_persons = persons
       - status = 'available'
       - created_at = current_timestamp
    3. Save to database (db.session.commit())
    4. Query all users WHERE role = 'volunteer'
    5. FOR EACH volunteer in volunteer_list:
       a. Create Notification:
          - user_id = volunteer.id
          - message = "New food available: {description}"
          - is_read = False
       b. Save notification
    6. Redirect to provider_dashboard with success message
END

Algorithm 2: Partial Food Pickup

ALGORITHM: PickFood
INPUT: food_post_id, persons_to_serve, needer_id
OUTPUT: Delivery created, food post updated

BEGIN
    1. Fetch food_post WHERE id = food_post_id
    2. IF food_post.remaining_persons < persons_to_serve:
       a. Return error "Not enough food available"
    3. Create Delivery record:
       - food_post_id = food_post_id
       - volunteer_id = current_user.id
       - needer_id = needer_id
       - persons_served = persons_to_serve
       - status = 'picked'
    4. Update food_post:
       - remaining_persons -= persons_to_serve
       - IF remaining_persons == 0:
           status = 'picked_up'
    5. Create notification for provider
    6. Create notification for needer
    7. Save all changes (db.session.commit())
    8. Redirect to volunteer_dashboard
END

Algorithm 3: Cancellation with Food Return

ALGORITHM: CancelRequest
INPUT: delivery_id
OUTPUT: Delivery cancelled, food returned to pool

BEGIN
    1. Fetch delivery WHERE id = delivery_id
    2. Fetch related food_post WHERE id = delivery.food_post_id

        return redirect(url_for('needer_dashboard'))
    
    if delivery.status in ['received', 'cancelled']:
        flash('Cannot cancel this delivery.', 'warning')
        return redirect(url_for('needer_dashboard'))
    
    # Return food to available pool
    food_post = FoodPost.query.get(delivery.food_post_id)
    food_post.remaining_persons += delivery.persons_served
    if food_post.status == 'picked_up':
        food_post.status = 'available'
    
    delivery.status = 'cancelled'
    
    # Notify volunteer
    notif = Notification(
        user_id=delivery.volunteer_id,
        message=f'Delivery #{delivery.id} cancelled by '
                f'receiver. {delivery.persons_served} '
                f'persons returned to available pool.'
    )
    db.session.add(notif)
    db.session.commit()
    
    flash('Request cancelled. Food returned to '
          'available pool.', 'info')
    return redirect(url_for('needer_dashboard'))

8.3 Limitations

While the system successfully achieves its core objectives, the following limitations are acknowledged:

  • No Real-Time Push Notifications: The current implementation uses AJAX polling (every 15 seconds) rather than WebSocket-based real-time push notifications. This introduces a slight delay in notification delivery.
  • No Mobile Application: The system is web-based only. A dedicated mobile application would improve accessibility for volunteers in the field.
  • No GPS/Location Integration: The system relies on text-based addresses. GPS integration would enable proximity-based food matching and route optimization.
  • No Image Upload: Food posts are text-only. Image support would help volunteers visually assess food type and quantity.
  • Single Server Deployment: The current architecture is single-server. For production scale, a distributed architecture would be needed.
  • No Analytics Dashboard: There is no admin panel with statistical insights on food saved, deliveries completed, or active users.
  • No Food Safety Tracking: The system does not track food preparation time or expiry, which is important for food safety compliance.
